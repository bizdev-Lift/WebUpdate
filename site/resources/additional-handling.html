Additional handling: the fee with four separate triggers.

Additional handling is not one charge. It is a family of charges, each with its own threshold, and a package can trip several at once. It also quietly resets your billable weight to a 40 lb minimum, which catches shippers who never think of themselves as heavy.

The rule nobody mentions

The 40 lb minimum billable weight

This is the part of additional handling that surprises people, and it is worth understanding before any of the individual triggers.

When a package attracts an additional handling charge, carriers apply a minimum billable weight of 40 lbs to that package for the purposes of the base transportation rate. It does not matter that the box weighs 12 lbs. Once additional handling applies, the base rate is calculated as though it weighs 40.

So the true cost of tripping an additional handling threshold is not just the accessorial fee itself. It is the accessorial fee plus the difference between what you would have paid at your real billable weight and what you now pay at 40 lbs. For a light, awkward package this second cost is frequently larger than the first.

It is also why "just ship it, it is only a few pounds" is bad advice for anything long or oddly shaped. The weight is not the problem. The shape is, and the weight gets rewritten because of it.

Domestic charges in full

Every trigger, and what to do about it

  1. Additional handling — weight

    Applies to any single package exceeding 50 lbs actual weight. This is the most straightforward of the four and the hardest to engineer around, because the product weighs what it weighs. Where it is worth attacking is order composition: two compliant 30 lb cartons frequently cost less than one 60 lb carton once the accessorial and the higher weight break are both counted. Run the comparison per SKU rather than assuming consolidation is cheaper, because in this band it often is not. Above roughly 70 lbs it is also worth testing the shipment against an LTL freight quote.

  2. Additional handling — length

    Applies when the longest side exceeds 48 in. This is the trigger that catches sporting goods, auto parts, furniture components and anything shipped in a long carton. It is worth measuring the carton rather than the product: an inch of packing material on each end is what pushes many boxes over the line. Where the product genuinely cannot be shortened, this becomes a negotiation item rather than an engineering one, and it is one of the more winnable accessorial concessions because carriers can predict your volume of it precisely.

  3. Additional handling — girth

    Applies when length plus girth exceeds 105 in, where girth is twice the width plus twice the height. This one catches people out because no single dimension looks unusual. A 40 × 20 × 16 in box has a longest side well under 48 in, but its length plus girth is 40 + 2(20) + 2(16) = 112 in, so it trips the charge anyway. Anyone quoting shipping at checkout from dimensions needs this calculation in the logic, not just a longest-side check.

  4. Additional handling — packaging

    Applies when the item is not fully encased in corrugated cardboard. Wrapped items, banded bundles, soft-sided packaging, wooden crates, metal cases and anything with an exposed surface all qualify. This is the one trigger that is almost entirely within your control, and it is often the cheapest to fix: over-boxing a canvas or wrapped item into a plain corrugated carton costs a fraction of the recurring surcharge. Worth auditing your packaging spec against this specifically.

  5. Large package / oversize

    Applies when length plus girth exceeds 130 in. UPS calls it Large Package, FedEx calls it Oversize, and the effect is the same: it replaces additional handling rather than stacking on top of it, and it carries its own minimum billable weight. If you are already paying additional handling on a box, pushing it slightly larger can move you into this bracket and cost considerably more, which makes carton sizing a genuinely commercial decision at this end of the range.

  6. Over maximum limits

    Applies over 150 lbs, over 108 in in length, or over 165 in length plus girth. Both carriers charge $1,950. This is not a surcharge in any normal sense, it is a penalty for tendering a package the network is not built to carry, and it is the hardest fee on this list to have reversed. The answer is never to negotiate it. The answer is to catch these shipments before they are tendered and route them to LTL freight, where the same load moves for a fraction of the cost. Any shipping system you run should flag over-limit packages at order entry.
